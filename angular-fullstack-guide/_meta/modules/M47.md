- **F1 (g).** `Backend Interview Study Guide - Data storage, behavioral & concurrency.md`, heading `## 9. Behavioral, and telling a story that lands`. Its opening says:
  - "The most common failure among strong engineers is not lacking material — it's a story that describes a situation in detail and then trails off without a result. Interviewers write down outcomes. If your story has no ending, the note says "unclear impact."";

  - "A negative result stated clearly beats a good result left implied.";

### 1. The behavioral round: what a story is evidence of, STAR with the weight on action and an explicit result, and a story bank · delta 640 (540 + 100 verification paragraph) · SEC-M47-01
- **Problem:** asked "tell me about a time you improved performance", a candidate spends ninety seconds on the product and the team, describes the work as "we", and stops without saying what changed. Another has one good story and stretches it over five prompts.
- **Model:** a **behavioral question** asks for a past situation, because a past action is evidence of how you work (our framing; C1). **STAR** (situation, task, action, result; no origin claimed, C2) with the weights F1 gives: situation and task in two sentences, action as the substance, and the result as the last sentence, stated explicitly even when negative (F1 quoted whole, lesson 3; canonical here, lesson 18). The **result sentence**: our term for that last sentence, with a number and its baseline where one exists. A **story bank** (our term): six to eight stories, each tagged with the prompts it answers (a performance win, a migration, an incident, a disagreement, a mistake, mentoring, a conflict with a product owner, a "why this role"), so one story is not stretched across five prompts and each prompt has a first choice. Practice: aloud, timed at two minutes, checking for the result sentence (F1's drill and F2's method, quoted).
- **Analogy:** a lab notebook entry: one line of setup, the procedure in detail, and the measured result at the bottom. A notebook entry without a result is not an experiment.
- **Diagram:** `flowchart LR`: prompt → pick a story from the bank → S and T (two sentences) → A (most of the time) → R (last sentence) → follow-ups (§7).
- **Failure modes with signals** (lesson 5; our suggestions): a story with no result (signal: "and what happened?"); a story told as "we" (signal: "what did *you* do?"); a long setup (signal: the interviewer interrupts before the action); one story stretched over every prompt (signal: "do you have a different example?").
- **Mechanism:** read from F1 why the result goes last and why a negative result stated clearly beats a good one left implied (F1's "unclear impact" sentence, quoted as the guide's claim, lesson 1). Read from F2 why to practise aloud ("retrieval and articulation, not recognition").
- **Code:** a `text` fence: one story outline in STAR with word counts per part (an example story, ours: an Angular form that lost drafts on session expiry, with example numbers), then a story-bank table in a `text` fence (story → prompts it answers → its result sentence). Steps: (1) write the result sentence first, because it is what the story is evidence of; (2) cut the situation to two sentences, because context past that delays the action; (3) mark each "we" and say which part was yours, because the follow-up will ask.
- **Callouts:** none required.
- **First-use definitions:** behavioral question, STAR, situation, task, action, result, result sentence, story bank, prompt.
- **Must state:** F1's structure paragraph quoted whole (two sentences; action the substance; the result "stated explicitly as the last thing you say") with §9 linked (lesson 16) and called the backend guide's advice; F1's "A negative result stated clearly beats a good result left implied"; F1's "unclear impact" sentence attributed to the guide; F2's aloud method quoted; STAR's origin not claimed (C2); the story bank of six to eight stories with prompt tags (ours); the four failure modes with signals; §9 linked and the seven §9 prompts not retold (Questions not asked).
- **Verification:** no lab (nothing observed); F1, F2 and advice worded as advice (C1).
- **Questions drilled:** Q47.01, Q47.02.
- **Misconceptions:**
  - "the situation is the interesting part". Cause: it is the part you remember best. Correction: the action is the evidence, and the situation needs only the two sentences that make the action understandable (F1).
  - "a story without a happy ending should be avoided". Cause: interviews feel like selling. Correction: a clearly stated negative result plus what you would change is a complete story (F1).
  - "say 'we' to sound like a team player". Cause: credit-sharing is a good habit at work. Correction: name the team once, then say what you did, because the round assesses your actions.
  - "memorize stories word for word". Cause: it feels safe. Correction: memorize the result sentence and the beats, and tell the rest freshly, because a recited story breaks at the first follow-up (§7).
- **The verification paragraph** (placeholder before §1, 80-110 words): "How this module was checked." Run: Vitest in Node 24 for Exercise 47.1 (characterization tests over our legacy function and two refactors), Exercise 47.2 (our list core in two designs) and Q47.09. ### 2. Four front-end stories: a performance win, a migration, a production incident and a disagreement over architecture · delta 620 · SEC-M47-02 (Opus)
- **Problem:** a front-end candidate tells backend-shaped stories. "We made the API faster" is told for a front-end role, an LCP improvement comes with no baseline or no word on whether it was field or lab data, a migration is a list of `ng update` commands, an incident story begins with the fix, and an architecture disagreement ends with "and they agreed with me".
- **Model:** four **story skeletons** (canonical here, lesson 18), each with the evidence that makes it credible. All are our suggestions, and each links its backend-guide counterpart once.

### 3. Legacy codebases and migrations without blame: the constraints of the time, how you learned the code before changing it, and words that explain instead of accuse · delta 540 · SEC-M47-03
- **Problem:** asked about a legacy AngularJS or Angular 8 app, a candidate says "the previous team wrote spaghetti", "nobody knew what they were doing" and "we had to rewrite everything". The interviewer hears how this person will talk about the interviewer's own code. Another candidate changes the legacy code, "fixes" a rounding quirk a report depended on, and breaks month-end totals.
- **Model:** a **blame-free account** (our term) describes the code through the constraints of its time: the version and tools available, the deadline, the team size and what the code still does well. It then says how you learned it before changing it and what you would do differently. F3's principle, applied by us to talking about code (lesson 9): describe contributing causes "without indicting any individual or team", and assume people "did the right thing with the information they had" (quoted whole, lesson 3). F4's Prime Directive says the same for retrospectives (quoted whole). **Learning before changing:** F1 §9.3's means ("reading, tracing requests, writing tests to probe behavior, talking to people"). A **characterization test** (F5: Wikipedia's definition quoted whole, "coined by Michael Feathers" as Wikipedia says) pins what the code does now, quirks included, so a refactor can prove that it kept them and a deliberate change is visible as one changed test (Exercise 47.1). The migration strategy itself is 39's (§9 strangler fig and hybrids, §10 program; linked once each; Q40.14 linked once for rewrite versus modernize).
- **Blame words to replace** (a `text` fence, our suggestion): "spaghetti" → "grew without a module boundary as features were added"; "they didn't test" → "the code had no tests, so we added characterization tests before changing it"; "stupid workaround" → "a workaround for <constraint> that is no longer needed since <version>"; "we had to rewrite" → "we chose to replace X because Y; we kept Z".
- **Analogy:** a building surveyor's report on an old house: it records that the wiring matches the code of its decade and says what to replace first. It does not call the electrician incompetent.
- **Diagram:** `flowchart LR`: legacy fact → constraint at the time → what it still does well → how you learned it (read, trace, characterization tests, people) → what you changed and kept → what you would do differently.
- **Mechanism:** read from F3 why blame hides problems ("people will not bring issues to light for fear of punishment", quoted with its condition) and why the fix is to systems ("You can't "fix" people…", quoted whole). The text says that applying this to an interview answer is our advice (lesson 9). Read from F5 why a characterization test pins current behavior instead of intended behavior. Read from F1 §9.3 that "What did you leave alone, and why?" is a follow-up to prepare.
- **Code:** an Excerpt of `exercise-1-legacy-refactor/exercise-1.test.ts` (one characterization case and the deliberately changed case), because it shows how a pinned quirk and a deliberate change look in a test. Steps: (1) the first test asserts what the legacy code returns now, even where it looks wrong, because the pin is a fact about the code, not a judgment; (2) the changed case names the old and the new result in its title, because a reviewer must see the change was intended. Link Exercise 47.1 (relink comment).
- **Callouts:** none required.

### 4. Live coding an Angular task: clarify, say the plan, build in working increments, narrate, and recover when stuck · delta 580 · SEC-M47-04
- **Problem:** given "build a searchable, paginated list in 40 minutes", a candidate types silently for ten minutes, scaffolds an NgModule and a service layer, nests a `subscribe` inside a `subscribe`, never runs the code until minute 35, never tries an empty result, and guesses an API instead of asking whether the docs are allowed.
- **Model:** a **live-coding round** is what 45 §1 calls machine coding: one component built live (handoff 45:35, linked once; Q45.02 not re-asked). The **live-coding loop** (canonical here, lesson 18; our suggestion):
  1. **clarify** (inputs, the data's shape and size, required states such as loading, empty and error, whether tests are expected, and whether docs and AI assistants are allowed, C5);
  2. **say the plan** in three or four lines;
  3. **build the smallest working version**, then add one behavior at a time, each running;
  4. **narrate decisions, not keystrokes**;
  5. **try the edges** (empty, one item, a page past the end, whitespace in the query);
  6. **check time at the halfway mark** and say what you will cut;
  7. **wrap up** with what you would do next.

  Modern Angular by default, because it is less code to type and read: a standalone component (15 §2), signals and `computed` for derived state (17 §3), `@for` with `track` (18 §2), and flattening operators instead of nested subscriptions when RxJS is used (23 §2, Q23.06 not re-asked); each linked once. When the task is a pure function or a kernel, test it first (handoff M46.md:118; Exercise 47.2). **When stuck** (our suggestion): say what you are trying, reduce the case, write the failing input down, and ask a specific question. Asking is not a penalty in our advice, and F1 §9.7's "ask people to repeat or rephrase" applies to the prompt itself.
- **Analogy:** a pilot's checklist read aloud: the co-pilot can follow, catch a skipped step and help. A silent pilot leaves the co-pilot nothing to assess.
- **Diagram:** `sequenceDiagram` with Candidate and Interviewer across the round: clarifying questions → plan → first running version → checkpoint ("does this match what you want?") → edges → time check → wrap-up.
- **Failure modes with signals** (lesson 5; our suggestions): silence (signal: the interviewer starts asking what you are doing); over-scaffolding (signal: nothing renders by the halfway mark); no edges tried (signal: the interviewer types an empty query); guessing an API (signal: a compile error you cannot explain, so ask whether the docs are allowed).
- **Mechanism:** our reasoning, worded as advice. The interviewer can credit only what they see and hear (45's precedent: the prep site's "never verbalized" sentence is about design rounds, so it is **not** reused here, REVIEW-M45 F-5). Increments matter because a running partial answer can be extended under follow-ups, while a non-running complete one cannot be assessed. Read from 29 §1 (owner) what a unit test of a pure core covers that a component test does not.
- **Code:** (1) a `text` fence: the narration script for each loop step (our suggestion, about one line each); (2) a `// Partial:` Angular snippet: a standalone list component with `input()`, `signal`, `computed` over Exercise 47.2's core, and `@for` with `track` (C7: each API checked in 22.2.1 typings; owners linked). Steps: (1) the core is a pure function, because it can be tested in seconds and survives any framework question; (2) the component only holds signals and calls the core, because derived state belongs in `computed` (17 §3). Link Exercise 47.2 (relink comment).
- **Callouts:** none required.
- **First-use definitions:** live-coding round, machine coding (link 45 §1), live-coding loop, increment, narration, edge case, checkpoint, wrap-up.
- **Must state:** live coding = 45 §1's machine coding, linked once (handoff 45:35); the seven-step loop (canonical); ask about docs and AI assistants, no rule stated (C5); modern defaults with 15 §2, 17 §3, 18 §2 and 23 §2 linked once each; the four failure modes with signals; test the kernel first (handoff M46.md:118); the prep-site sentence not reused (REVIEW-M45 F-5); no platform named as standard (C4); Exercise 47.2 linked.
- **Verification:** the Partial snippet's APIs read in `labs/angular/node_modules/@angular/core` 22.2.1 typings (read only; LOG names the files); the rest is advice.
- **Questions drilled:** Q47.10, Q47.11; practised in Exercise 47.2.
- **Misconceptions:**
  - "the interviewer wants to see you code fast". Cause: the clock is visible. Correction: in our advice the round assesses how you reach a working answer and explain it, so a stated plan and a running increment beat speed.
  - "asking questions looks weak". Cause: school tests forbid it. Correction: clarifying the prompt is part of the task, and a wrong assumption costs more time than a question.
  - "show every Angular feature you know". Cause: it looks thorough. Correction: the smallest modern solution is easier to finish and to explain; a store, a service layer or an NgModule for one component costs minutes and adds nothing (28 §11).
  - "if stuck, keep quiet until it works". Cause: struggling feels embarrassing. Correction: saying what you are trying lets the interviewer help and gives them reasoning to credit.

### 5. Take-home assignments: scope and time-box, what the repository must show, the README, and the review call · delta 500 · SEC-M47-05
- **Problem:** a candidate spends twenty hours on a "four-hour" take-home, adds NgRx, SSR and Playwright to a two-screen app, ships no README, leaves one giant commit and a failing test, and in the review call cannot explain a library choice.
- **Model:** a **take-home** is a scoped task done alone and reviewed later, usually with a call (our description, C1). Our suggestions:
  - **Scope**: confirm the time box and what "done" means, then do less, well: the required features with loading, empty and error states (37 §10 linked once);
  - **time-box**: stop at the agreed time and list the rest;
  - **what the repository must show**: it runs from a clean clone with one command; the tests pass; the commit history tells the story in steps; the Angular code is modern by default; no secrets;
  - **the README** (canonical here; our suggestion): how to run and test, what is implemented, decisions and trade-offs (each as a complete statement, 45 §2 linked), what you would do with more time, and the time spent;
  - **the review call**: walk through one decision, accept a change request live, and say what you would do differently.

  Over-building is a cost: a store library in a two-screen app is Q28.32's trade-off (linked once). End-to-end tests are worth one journey at most (30 §1, linked once).
- **Analogy:** a contractor's quote for a small job: a clear scope, a fixed price, what is excluded, and a site left clean. Gold-plating a shed does not win the house.
- **Diagram:** `timeline`: brief received → questions sent → time-box set → core with tests → states and edges → README → submit → review call.
- **Failure modes with signals** (lesson 5; our suggestions): overrunning the time box (signal: you are adding a library at hour three); no README (signal: you cannot say in one line how to run it); untestable code (signal: the only tests are a generated `should create`); unexplained choices (signal: in the call, "why this library?" has no answer).
- **Mechanism:** our reasoning, worded as advice. A reviewer sees only the repository and the README, so a decision not written down does not exist for them. F1 §9.5's "have the mechanism ready" applies in the review call (quoted). Read from 29 §1 (owner) which tests a small app needs.
- **Code:** a `text` fence: the README skeleton (headings and one example line each, ours). Steps: (1) run and test commands first, because a reviewer who cannot run the code stops; (2) decisions with their costs, because a trade-off without a cost reads as advocacy (Q40.13's trap, linked); (3) time spent, because it frames scope honestly.
- **Callouts:** none required.
- **First-use definitions:** take-home, time box, definition of done, README, commit history, review call, gold-plating.
- **Must state:** scope and time-box as advice; the README contents (canonical); a clean clone runs with one command; loading, empty and error states (37 §10 linked once); Q28.32, 30 §1, 29 §1 and 45 §2 linked once each; no time or pay data claimed (C6); AI-assistant use only as "ask, and say when you used it" (C5); failure modes with signals.
- **Verification:** no lab; advice worded as advice.
- **Questions drilled:** Q47.12, Q47.13.
- **Misconceptions:**
  - "more features show more skill". Cause: more looks impressive. Correction: a reviewer sees a missed time box and less-tested code; a small, tested core with written trade-offs is easier to judge well.
  - "the README is optional if the code is clean". Cause: good code feels self-explanatory. Correction: the reasons and the cut scope are not in the code, and the reviewer reads the README first.
  - "the take-home is done when submitted". Cause: it is a deliverable. Correction: the review call is part of the task, so plan what you will walk through.
  - "use the newest experimental API to stand out". Cause: novelty signals currency. Correction: an API marked experimental or in developer preview needs its status said and a reason ([Q39.05](../../modules/39-version-history-and-migrations.md#q39-05) for what each status promises).

### 7. Under the hood: how one story is drilled into, numbers you can defend, and telling a design or a migration as your own project · delta 580 · SEC-M47-07 (Opus)
- **Problem:** a candidate's performance story survives the first telling, then collapses at "how did you measure that?" and "what would you do differently?". A "40% faster" is a number they cannot source. A system design they built (46) is told as a lecture instead of a story. The "why Angular" answer is a pitch with no costs.
- **Model:** the **follow-up ladder** (our term, canonical here): each rung asks for more specific evidence.
  1. what did *you* do;
  2. how do you know it worked (the measurement and its kind: field or lab, 31 §1);
  3. what it cost (complexity, time, money; F1 §9.5's follow-up);
  4. what else could have caused the change (F1 §9.5: "How did you know that change caused the improvement and not something else?");
  5. what you would do differently;
  6. what happened after you left or moved on.

  A **defensible number**: one you can source (a dashboard, a report, a test), with its baseline and its kind. If you cannot reconstruct it, "say so and describe the method rather than inflating the number" (F1 §9.5, quoted). **Telling a design as your own project** (handoffs 45:1225, 46:2185, 46:2186): 46 §1's six parts become the Action of a STAR story (linked once), and the trade-off statements are 45 §2's (linked). **The "why Angular" story** (handoff 40:1369): Q40.13 owns its structure (linked, not restated); told as your decision, it adds what you weighed then and what you would weigh now. **Second language** (C12): F1 §9.7's "ask people to repeat or rephrase" and F2's practice aloud, quoted as the guide's.
- **Analogy:** a witness under cross-examination: a well-prepared witness has the receipts for every claim and says "I don't know" when they don't.
- **Diagram:** `flowchart TB`: the six rungs from "what did you do?" down to "what happened after?", each with the evidence it needs on the right.
- **Mechanism:** read from F1 §9.5 why an unsupported metric is worse than none (quoted as the guide's), and from 31 §1 why a number without its kind cannot be compared. Our reasoning on the ladder: every rung below the first is a check on the rung above, so prepare each story down to rung 4 at least.
- **Code:** a `text` fence: one example story (ours, example numbers labeled) with the six rungs and a prepared answer for each, including an honest "I don't know" with the method. Steps: (1) source every number before the round, because rung 2 asks for it; (2) prepare the "what else" rung, because a confounder you name yourself is credited, and one the interviewer names is not; (3) keep one "I would do differently" per story, because rung 5 is worth preparing for every story (our suggestion).
- **Callouts:** none required.
- **First-use definitions:** follow-up ladder, rung, defensible number, confounder, project story.
- **Must state:** the six rungs (canonical); F1 §9.5's "How did you know that change caused the improvement and not something else?" and "say so and describe the method rather than inflating the number", quoted; field or lab named for numbers (31 §1, lesson 10); 46 §1 linked once as the design-to-story mapping (handoffs 45:1225, 46:2185, 46:2186); Q40.13 linked, not restated (handoff 40:1369); F1 §9.7 and F2 quoted for the second language (C12); 45 §2 linked once.
- **Verification:** no lab; F1, F2 and owners.
- **Questions drilled:** Q47.15.
- **Misconceptions:**
  - "a precise number sounds more credible". Cause: precision signals rigor. Correction: a precise number you cannot source falls at rung 2, and F1 says an unsupported metric is worse than none.
  - "follow-ups mean the story failed". Cause: they feel like doubt. Correction: they are how the evidence is checked, and a prepared story invites them.
  - "a system design story should show the whole design". Cause: the design took months. Correction: told as a story, the design is the Action, at the depth of the one decision that mattered (46 §1's hard step).
  - "'I don't know' ends the answer". Cause: it sounds like failing. Correction: "I don't know the exact figure; here is how we measured it" keeps the method, which is what rung 2 checks.
